<- Introduction

Introduction

In the following report, I will discuss the results of the analyses carried out on the Art Interest (AI) scale of the VAIAK.

The AI scale is composed by 11 items. These are either about subjective interest (e.g., I am interested in art) or behaviors (e.g., I go to visit art exhibitions). Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale. Items of subjective interest are rated from “not at all” (1) to “very much” (7), while behavioral items range from “less than once a year” (1) to “once a week or more often” (7). Only one scale is consider, and no subscale is measured.

You can find the content of the items here.

Only participants who have completed the complete scale are taken into account.

Sample

2828 participants were under the age of 18, so I excluded them.

The final number of participants is 17672, with an average age of 30.9 years, \(sd\) = 13.85. For 2585 participants we did not have the age. For the gender the sample is composed by 11634 women, 7190 men and 1826 people that define themselves neither as woman or man.

Many participants were lay people (N = 6134) with an important number of art experts as well (N = 2133). For the majority we do not have the expertise level (N = 9405).

The following graph represent the distribution of the score, the line is the mean (44.53). The AI ranges from 11 to 77.

Internal reliability

McDonald’s \(\omega\), which is considered acceptable when above .7, was calculated and show a good internal reliability (\(\omega\) = 0.94).

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

For the CFA, I will keep in mind that a good fit is usually judged as such when the following criteria are respected:

  • CFI > .9
  • TLI > .9
  • RMSEA < .8
  • SRMR < .8

Item Responce Theory (IRT)

I carry out two distinctive IRT. One for the entire scale, the other with the two subfactors (interest_subjective and interest_behaviour) divided.

One factor model

This graph represent how informative on average the items are in relation to the ability of the participants (\(\theta\)).

We can notice that it is in general pretty normally distributed around 0, with a little skewness into the lower values. This means that it is more informative for the people with lower ability.

For each item, \(a\) represents the discrimination parameter, i.e. how much the value vary in relation to the ability \(\theta\). A high \(a\) means that the probability of answering an item correctly changes very sharply as \(\theta\) increases.

\(b\) represents how much ability \(\theta\) the participants needs to pass from a responce (e.g. 1) to the next (2). For example, in AI_1_1 a participant with an ability of least 0.08 (b5) has a 50% probability of responding 6 or higher. The higher the \(b\) the more difficult it is for a participant to give that answer.
